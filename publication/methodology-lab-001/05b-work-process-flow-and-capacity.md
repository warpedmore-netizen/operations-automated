# Work, process, flow and capacity

## Understand how work really moves

Process documentation describes intended activity; operational analysis must also reveal actual demand, decisions, waiting, rework, exceptions and informal recovery. The objective is not a perfect diagram. It is an evidence-based account of how work moves and why outcomes vary.

## Segment work before measuring it

Group work where the grouping changes treatment. Useful distinctions may include:

- standard, complex and exceptional cases;
- planned, unplanned and urgent work;
- value demand and failure demand;
- new work, rework, recovery and maintenance;
- high-consequence and low-consequence decisions;
- different channels, user needs or product types; and
- work requiring scarce skill, specialist advice or physical assets.

One average across unlike work can produce poor staffing, misleading performance and unsafe automation.

## Map the current flow

For each significant work type, trace:

- trigger and entry conditions;
- information and materials received;
- classification and priority;
- activities and decisions;
- active work and elapsed waiting;
- queues, batch points and work-in-progress;
- hand-offs, dependencies and acceptance criteria;
- checks, approvals and controls;
- exceptions, rework and failure recovery;
- completion, confirmation and downstream effects; and
- measures and ownership.

Observe work directly where practical and compare the documented route with actual cases. Record variation rather than forcing every case into a single happy path.

## Diagnose flow

Look for causes that shape the whole system:

- uncontrolled arrival of work or unclear entry criteria;
- prioritisation that changes faster than the work can respond;
- high work-in-progress and context switching;
- specialist constraints or approval bottlenecks;
- batching and calendar-driven delay;
- missing or poor-quality information;
- repeated checking caused by low trust or unclear control;
- rework and failure demand;
- local utilisation targets that lengthen end-to-end time;
- dependencies with mismatched service or completion conditions; and
- technology that accelerates activity without improving the complete flow.

Use cause-and-effect hypotheses and test them. A visible queue may be a symptom of demand shaping, policy, allocation, skill, system reliability or downstream blocking.

## Match demand and capacity

Assess demand by type, volume, arrival pattern, effort and variability. Assess effective capacity by required skill, availability, interruption, coordination, maintenance and improvement need. Use ranges where precision is unjustified.

Consider:

- peak and tail behaviour, not only averages;
- service expectations and tolerable waiting;
- the effect of utilisation on queues and recovery;
- resilience for absence, incidents and demand shocks;
- scarce shared resources;
- work displaced to other teams or users; and
- capacity required to improve the system itself.

Options include shaping demand, changing entry conditions, removing failure demand, pooling or protecting capacity, reducing work-in-progress, simplifying decisions, improving information, cross-skilling, changing schedules or adding justified capacity.

## Redesign principles

Redesign in this order:

1. remove work that does not need to exist;
2. correct policy, information or failure conditions creating avoidable work;
3. simplify the journey and decision;
4. organise work around outcomes and sensible end-to-end ownership;
5. reduce queues, hand-offs and work-in-progress where they do not add justified value;
6. protect necessary specialist independence and control;
7. define exception and recovery routes; and
8. add technology only where the redesigned work and boundary justify it.

## Measures

Use a balanced set:

- outcome and first-time completion;
- demand and failure-demand pattern;
- end-to-end elapsed time and active work time;
- work-in-progress, queue age and blocked work;
- quality, rework, defects and exceptions;
- capacity, workload and interruption;
- user effort and recovery;
- cost and resource use; and
- control, resilience and learning.

Measures should support decisions, not become the purpose of work. Check for gaming, displaced failure and unmeasured groups.

## Outputs and gate

Produce segmented demand evidence, current work-and-decision flow, capacity and constraint assessment, causal hypotheses, target flow, exception and recovery design, measures and an improvement experiment or implementation backlog.

Proceed when the target improves the end-to-end outcome under realistic demand. Do not automate a flow whose exceptions, information, ownership or capacity are materially misunderstood.
