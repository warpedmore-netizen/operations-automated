# Foundations – purpose, value, principles and accountability

## Purpose before mechanism

Operational work exists to create or protect an outcome. Begin with the reason for the work, not the process name, system boundary or favoured technology. Ask:

- Whose situation should be different when the operation succeeds?
- What are they trying to achieve?
- What minimum outcome must still be protected when the ideal is not possible?
- Which outcomes would be unacceptable even if local targets were met?
- Why is intervention justified now?

Purpose should be specific enough to guide trade-offs. “Improve efficiency” is not sufficient because it does not say whose effort, which resource, what outcome or what displaced consequence matters.

## User-defined and organisational value

Value has at least two connected perspectives:

- **user-defined value:** the useful outcome, experience, access, confidence, fairness or recovery that matters to the recipient; and
- **organisational value:** the sustainable capability, income, affordability, obligation, resilience, learning or strategic progress that enables continued delivery.

Neither perspective automatically dominates. A design that delights one user but creates unsustainable cost may fail; a design that lowers internal unit cost by increasing user failure, exclusion or future demand may also fail. Record the trade-off and the authority to resolve it.

Useful value definitions contain an outcome, beneficiary, time or condition, minimum acceptable result and evidence. Treat benefits as hypotheses until observed in use.

## Human-led automation

Technology should serve an authorised human purpose. Capability does not create permission. The method therefore requires deliberate choices about:

- what should remain human because judgement, empathy, tacit knowledge, physical presence or accountable discretion matters;
- what technology may support or execute;
- which inputs, actions and outcomes are permitted;
- where review, approval, stop and recovery controls sit; and
- who remains accountable for consequential outcomes.

Manual work is not automatically a failure state. It should still have a purpose, owner, capacity, information, control, continuity and improvement route. Automation is not automatically progress. It may scale unclear decisions, hide failure or create brittle dependencies.

## Responsibility, authority and accountability

Use the words precisely:

- **responsibility** is the obligation to perform an assigned contribution competently and honestly;
- **authority** is the legitimate permission to decide, commit resources, accept exposure or act; and
- **accountability** is answerability for the consequential outcome within a human or organisational system.

AI can be responsible for the quality and faithful execution of its assigned contribution. It cannot currently hold human, organisational, legal or moral accountability, accept risk or create its own authority. An authorised human or organisation remains accountable for consequential decisions and outcomes.

This does not mean the person should repeat the analysis. AI should do the work it can, form a view, expose its reasoning and make review decision-ready.

## Meaningful human control

A human click is not meaningful control. The reviewer needs:

- relevant evidence and uncertainty;
- enough time for the consequence involved;
- suitable competence or access to advice;
- actual authority to change the outcome; and
- a practical route to challenge, condition, reject, pause, stop or recover.

If those conditions are absent, redesign the control. Adding another approval to an overloaded person can increase delay without improving assurance.

## Evidence and challenge

Separate important claims into recorded evidence, human judgement, AI inference, assumption and recommendation. Reconstruct the strongest reasonable contextual meaning before challenging it. Use a reverse, boundary, transfer, stakeholder, contrary-evidence, time-horizon, failure or authority test when the result could change.

Do not manufacture disagreement for display. State where evidence changed the assessment, where disagreement remains and what is uncertain.

## Learning through failure

Failure can create evidence, but it is not automatically valuable or acceptable. Bound experiments so that likely harm, cost and reversibility are proportionate to the learning need. Define expected behaviour, failure conditions, stop conditions, monitoring, recovery and the decision that the evidence will inform.

Do not call uncontrolled harm a learning experiment. Do not preserve a failed design because delivery effort has already been spent.

## Practical foundation output

At minimum, record:

- purpose and intended outcome;
- primary beneficiary and affected stakeholders;
- user and organisational value;
- minimum and unacceptable outcomes;
- evidence, judgement, assumptions and uncertainty;
- responsibility, authority and accountability;
- relevant obligations and specialist interfaces; and
- next governed action.

These foundations should remain visible throughout design and operation. Revisit them when evidence, demand, strategy or obligation changes.
