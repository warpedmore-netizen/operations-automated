# People, organisation, capability and culture

## Treat people as participants, not implementation variables

People understand context, create relationships, exercise judgement, carry tacit knowledge and experience the effects of operating design. They are also subject to workload, incentives, power, uncertainty and change fatigue. A responsible methodology designs the social and technical system together.

This chapter identifies operational questions. Formal employment, consultation, reward, health, safety and people-policy decisions remain with authorised People specialists and leaders.

## Understand the current people system

For material roles, examine:

- purpose and expected outcomes;
- responsibilities, authority and accountability;
- actual work, including invisible coordination and recovery;
- demand, workload, capacity, scheduling and interruptions;
- skills, experience, accreditation and tacit knowledge;
- access to information, tools, advice and escalation;
- incentives, targets and conflicting expectations;
- team boundaries, collaboration and dependency;
- physical, cognitive and emotional demands;
- inclusion, accessibility, fairness and voice;
- turnover, absence, resilience and key-person dependency; and
- previous change, trust and learning conditions.

Compare formal role descriptions with observed work. Important operational labour often sits in informal workarounds, relationship maintenance, exception handling and translation between systems.

## Capacity is more than headcount

Usable capacity depends on skill, work mix, variation, interruptions, coordination, tools and recovery time. Avoid simple conclusions based only on average volume or full utilisation.

Understand:

- demand by work type and time pattern;
- effective time available for each capability;
- planned, unplanned and improvement work;
- constraints created by scarce skills or approvals;
- queue and service effects of high utilisation;
- resilience for absence, peaks and incidents; and
- work shifted to users, managers or other teams.

Make priority conflicts explicit. Adding work without removing, delaying or resourcing something else is not a plan.

## Design roles and authority

Target roles should make clear:

- the outcome the role serves;
- the decisions it may take and limits on that authority;
- work retained by people and work supported or executed by technology;
- information and evidence required;
- collaboration and specialist interfaces;
- exception, override, escalation and stop rights;
- measures that do not encourage harmful local optimisation; and
- learning, support and continuity arrangements.

Do not leave people nominally accountable for automated outcomes they cannot understand, influence or stop.

## Capability development and transfer

Capability includes knowledge, skill, confidence, relationships, tools, decision practice and the ability to recover. Design development around real tasks and decisions, not only training attendance.

A transfer plan should show:

- current and required capability;
- who must learn what, and why;
- supported practice and feedback;
- access to examples, instructions and expert help;
- how competence or confidence will be evidenced;
- ownership after delivery; and
- how knowledge survives role or supplier change.

The receiving operation should be able to run, challenge, maintain, recover and improve the new arrangement without unnecessary permanent dependence on the delivery team.

## Culture as repeated conditions

Avoid treating culture as a slogan or an individual attitude problem. Examine the repeated conditions that shape behaviour: leadership action, incentives, decision rights, workload, psychological safety, consequence of speaking up, response to failure, information access and whether improvement suggestions lead to action.

If the desired behaviour conflicts with measures, rewards or authority, change the operating conditions rather than relying on communication alone.

## Change and adoption

Involve affected people early enough to shape the design. Distinguish consultation, co-design, formal decision and communication. Explain what will change, what will not, why, when, how work and roles are affected, what support exists and how concerns can change the outcome.

Observe actual use after release. Low adoption may indicate poor fit, missing capacity, unsafe design, weak trust, inaccessible delivery or an unaddressed incentive—not merely resistance.

## Outputs and tests

Produce a role-and-capability view, capacity assessment, people-impact and fairness assessment, authority map, capability-transfer plan and adoption evidence plan.

Test whether people can perform the work under realistic demand, understand technology boundaries, exercise review and stop rights, reach support, recover from failure and influence improvement. Escalate material employment, safety, fairness or consultation questions to the appropriate authority.
