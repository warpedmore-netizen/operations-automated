## 1. Executive assessment

The research does not support a technology-first conclusion. Across public-sector agentic guidance, cybersecurity codes, adoption surveys, software-delivery research, FinOps practice and financial-sector resilience rules, the repeated operational message is that capability must be connected to purpose, bounded authority, observable execution, recoverable failure, measurable value and retained human accountability.

The most important operational problem is **untraceable delegation and dependency**: organisations can give an agent, AI-enabled workflow, internally created application or supplier the practical ability to affect an outcome without maintaining a usable line from the outcome to its owner, permissions, costs, dependencies, runtime evidence, stop route and observed value.

Operations Automated already has a strong defence. The approved v0.7 readiness path explicitly requires an agent goal, identity, least privilege, approval gates, monitoring, stop conditions, a kill switch, rollback and incident ownership. Its outside-in method, connected-work model, interface contracts, Test stage and learning loop also require outcome ownership, dependencies, adverse-condition testing, recovery and retained learning. The research strengthens these positions rather than contradicting them.

The principal weakness is depth and operability. The approved coverage model itself labels financial cost and benefits, suppliers and concentration, inventories, communication and several lifecycle practices as only Identified or Outlined. The AI guide acknowledges missing evaluation packs, operational monitoring, provider guidance, multi-agent validation and build-buy-integrate-exit guidance. A practitioner can see what good must contain, but cannot yet consistently produce, test and assure the required records.

The highest-priority challenge is **CH-001: turn agentic readiness into an operable lifecycle and runtime assurance profile**. It should not become a separate methodology: it should make the existing readiness and OPERATE controls usable from inventory and design through runtime, change, incident, offboarding and retirement.

The strongest initial commercial opportunity is a facilitated **AI and Agent Operational Assurance Diagnostic**. It uses Operations Automated's genuine strengths—outside-in value, readiness, authority, connected risk and recoverable implementation—while avoiding a generic AI strategy service. A software feature or certification claim would be premature.

There is no previous formal pack against which to claim a monthly trend. Compared with the evidence embedded in v0.7, the material delta is that 2026 guidance now treats agent identity, bounded autonomy, human checkpoints, runtime logs, kill switches, third-party agents and lifecycle retirement as practical deployment concerns; FinOps evidence makes per-outcome AI unit economics a mainstream operational issue; and UK financial rules make structured third-party registers and incident reporting a near-term implementation requirement for in-scope firms.

**Overall verdict:** Operations Automated adequately frames the control problem and partially handles its application. It does not require material redesign on current evidence, but it does require material implementation guidance and validation.

### Agentic AI governance, security and accountability

**S01 — [IMDA Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI and May 2026 update](https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/factsheets/2026/updated-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai)**
Published by Singapore's Infocomm Media Development Authority; original version 22 January 2026, updated 20 May 2026. Government model framework for organisations developing or deploying agents, with more than 60 organisations contributing feedback and more than ten case studies. It addresses bounded autonomy and access, meaningful human accountability, lifecycle controls, continuous testing and monitoring, third-party and multi-agent risks, transparency and training. **Credibility: High for current government-backed practice guidance; Medium as outcome evidence.** It is voluntary, Singapore-led and partly informed by technology providers; case studies show practice, not causal effectiveness.

**S02 — [Government of Canada Guide on the Use of Agentic Artificial Intelligence](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/guide-use-agentic-artificial-intelligence.html)**
Current Government of Canada operational guidance accessed 1 August 2026; the retrieved page did not expose a publication date. Intended for federal departments and agencies. It defines before/during/after controls including named accountability, reversibility, realistic and adversarial testing, understandable tool/action/approval logs, drift monitoring, an external kill switch, changed-permission reassessment, offboarding and safe retirement. **Credibility: High for its intended public-sector context.** It is prescriptive guidance without a research sample, and legal and policy applicability outside Canada is limited.

**S03 — [NIST NCCoE concept paper on software and AI agent identity and authorisation](https://www.nccoe.nist.gov/sites/default/files/2026-02/accelerating-the-adoption-of-software-and-ai-agent-identity-and-authorization-concept-paper.pdf)**
Published 5 February 2026 for public comment. It frames identification, authentication, least privilege, dynamic authorisation, delegated authority, binding human and agent identities, tamper-evident auditing and non-repudiation as unresolved implementation questions. **Credibility: Medium.** NIST is authoritative, but this is a concept paper seeking evidence, not a completed standard or proof that one control architecture is effective. Its uncertainty is itself relevant evidence that agent identity practice remains immature.

**S04 — [UK AI Cyber Security Code of Practice](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-cyber-security-code-of-practice)**
Published by DSIT with NCSC on 31 January 2025 after a 2024 call for views. It provides baseline lifecycle security principles and an implementation guide; the UK intends it to inform an ETSI technical specification. **Credibility: High for UK government cyber guidance.** It is voluntary, cyber-focused and not an agentic governance or organisational-value framework.

**S05 — [NIST AI RMF Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)**
Published July 2024 as a cross-sector companion to the voluntary AI RMF. It calls for inventories of third parties with content access, updated procurement due diligence, monitoring of third-party AI, documented fallbacks, incident plans, rehearsal, continuous monitoring, manual fallback where appropriate and contractual notification/support expectations. **Credibility: High for transparent, authoritative risk guidance.** It is a voluntary GenAI profile, not agent-specific regulation or proof of realised outcomes.

### AI adoption, software creation, cost and operational value

**S06 — [UK DSIT AI Adoption Research](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research)**
Updated 13 February 2026; fieldwork ran from 12 February to 2 May 2025. It used a weighted telephone survey of 3,500 UK private-sector businesses with at least five employees and 100 follow-up qualitative interviews. Sixteen per cent reported using at least one AI technology; among adopters, 56% reported increased employee productivity while 77% reported no revenue change. The report states that many qualitative participants had no formal attribution measures and that the survey cannot detect shadow AI. **Credibility: High.** Limitations include self-report, excluded public/health/education sectors, a technology-aware respondent, adoption-definition sensitivity and fieldwork lag.

**S07 — [DORA 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development](https://research.google/pubs/dora-2025-state-of-ai-assisted-software-development-report/)**
Published by DORA/Google in 2025 using more than 100 hours of qualitative evidence and survey responses from nearly 5,000 technology professionals globally. It concludes that AI tends to amplify existing organisational strengths and weaknesses; the detailed report associates AI with higher throughput but continuing delivery instability and identifies user focus, version control, internal platforms, data and small batches as important enabling capabilities. **Credibility: Medium.** The sample is large and the method is documented, but it is observational and self-selected, the raw data are not public, sponsors sell technology, and association is not causation.

**S08 — [METR early-2025 developer productivity randomised controlled trial](https://metr.org/Early_2025_AI_Experienced_OS_Devs_Study-paper.pdf)**
Published 10 July 2025. Sixteen experienced open-source developers completed 246 randomly assigned tasks with or without early-2025 AI tools; AI access increased measured completion time by 19% in this narrow setting, despite participants forecasting a speed-up. **Credibility: High for the tested setting; Medium for transfer to organisational AI value.** The causal design is strong but the sample, repositories, tasks, participants and tools are narrow and time-sensitive.

**S09 — [METR February 2026 productivity-study update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)**
Published 24 February 2026. METR reports that its later experiment was biased by AI-dependent participants declining no-AI tasks, different pay and unreliable multi-agent timing. It considers improved 2026 productivity plausible but says the evidence is weak. **Credibility: Medium.** The value lies in transparent limitation and temporal warning, not a stable effect estimate.

**S10 — [State of FinOps 2026](https://data.finops.org/)**
The sixth annual global FinOps community survey included 1,192 respondents representing more than US$83 billion in annual cloud spend. Ninety-eight per cent of respondents said they now manage AI spend; visibility, allocation and value/ROI were leading difficulties, and granular AI spend plus unit economics were prominent tooling needs. **Credibility: Medium.** It is a large practitioner signal but self-selected, strongly enterprise/FinOps-oriented and not representative of all organisations.

**S11 — [FinOps for AI: Tools and Services Considerations](https://www.finops.org/wg/finops-for-ai-tools-services-considerations/)**
Published by the FinOps Foundation in April 2026 as practitioner guidance. It explains that one agent interaction can create many model calls and context costs, and recommends measuring total cost per business outcome rather than tokens alone. **Credibility: Medium.** It is operationally specific but consensus/practitioner guidance rather than independent outcome research; contributors may have provider interests.

**S12 — [Token Economics: Managing AI Value in SaaS Model Token Costs](https://www.finops.org/wg/token-economics-saas/)**
Published by the FinOps Foundation in July 2026. It recommends inventorying provider accounts, API keys and payment methods, adding attribution data, governing keys, selecting models proportionately and reporting cost per query, user or workflow. **Credibility: Medium.** It is current practitioner guidance in an immature measurement field, not a binding standard or causal study.

### Third-party concentration, critical dependencies and resilience

**S13 — [UK Critical Third Parties rules](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/ctps4) and [Bank of England supervisory statement SS6/24](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/supervisory-statement/2024/ss624-november-2024.pdf)**
Final UK financial-sector regime published November 2024, with rules effective from 1 January 2025 when a provider is designated. It requires governance, supply-chain and key nth-party risk management, end-to-end mapping, maximum tolerable disruption, scenario testing, incident playbooks, communication and learning. **Credibility: High and authoritative for designated UK financial critical third parties.** It is sector- and designation-specific; applying the same depth elsewhere would be an inference.

**S14 — [FCA PS26/2: Operational incident and third-party reporting](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/policy-statements/ps26-2-operational-incident-third-party-reporting) and [FCA implementation announcement](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/news-stories/fca-confirms-new-incident-and-third-party-rules-bolster-resilience)**
Published 18 March 2026 after consultation; rules take effect 18 March 2027 for specified UK financial firms. It standardises incident reporting and requires annual registers plus notification of new or materially changed third-party arrangements. The FCA's accompanying announcement reports that more than 40% of cyber incidents reported to it in 2025 involved a third party. **Credibility: High for the regulatory requirement and regulator-reported incident share.** Applicability is restricted to in-scope firms; the incident statistic reflects reports to the FCA, not all sectors.

**S15 — [EU Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554)**
Adopted 14 December 2022 and applicable from 17 January 2025. It requires in-scope financial entities to retain responsibility, maintain ICT third-party registers, assess concentration and subcontracting, test resilience proportionately and manage contractual and exit conditions. **Credibility: High; binding EU law for its scope.** It is not general cross-sector guidance and avoids universal concentration caps.

**S16 — [Financial Stability Board third-party risk toolkit](https://www.fsb.org/uploads/P041223-1.pdf)**
Published 4 December 2023 after public consultation, stakeholder outreach and a survey of practices, challenges and supervisory expectations. Its tools cover critical-service identification, relationship and nth-party mapping, due diligence, ongoing monitoring, timely incident information, continuity, exit and cross-authority coordination. **Credibility: High for international financial-policy consensus.** It is a non-binding, financial-sector toolkit designed for flexible, proportionate use.

## 4. Underlying problem analysis

| Finding | Visible symptom | Immediate and likely root cause | Contributing conditions | Operational implications |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RF-01 | An agent can act more broadly than users expect | Immediate: scope and approvals are vague. Root: technical capability is mistaken for delegated authority. | Novelty pressure, unclear consequence thresholds, ceremonial review | **People:** reviewers cannot intervene meaningfully. **Process:** no action classes. **Technology:** excessive tools/data. **Governance/risk:** no authorised boundary or retained acceptance. |
| RF-02 | Agents use shared or human credentials and actions cannot be attributed | Immediate: identity and access are inherited from applications or users. Root: no lifecycle model for non-human delegated actors. | Immature standards, multi-agent delegation, dynamic tool acquisition | **People:** accountability becomes disputed. **Process:** joiner/mover/leaver controls omit agents. **Technology:** revocation and intent evidence are weak. **Supplier:** identities cross platforms. **Risk:** privilege persists unnoticed. |
| RF-03 | Logs exist but harmful action is detected late or cannot be stopped | Immediate: logging, monitoring and recovery were treated as technical aftercare. Root: runtime operation was not designed as part of the service. | Model/tool changes, automation bias, weak incident practice, sensitive logs | **People:** operators lack skill and escalation. **Process:** no tested stop/recovery route. **Technology:** drift and tool calls are not observable. **Governance:** paper policy cannot constrain runtime. |
| RF-04 | Leadership's AI list and spend totals do not match real use | Immediate: self-service tools and embedded features bypass intake. Root: asset, identity, procurement, expense and data controls are disconnected. | Free tools, individual cards, embedded AI, unclear definitions | **People:** hidden workarounds and inconsistent training. **Process:** duplicated intake and assurance. **Technology/supplier:** unknown providers, keys and data flows. **Risk:** shadow use and unsupported dependencies. |
| RF-05 | Teams report time saved but cannot show an improved service or financial result | Immediate: adoption and activity proxies replace baselines and outcome measures. Root: value was not defined at use-case level before adoption. | Executive pressure, attribution difficulty, benefits outside revenue, no counterfactual | **People:** effort moves into checking or rework. **Process:** local gain transfers cost downstream. **Finance/governance:** investment cannot be compared or stopped. |
| RF-06 | More internal software is produced while stability, maintainability or support worsens | Immediate: code generation outruns design, review, testing and operations. Root: software is treated as output rather than a service asset with an owner and lifecycle. | Variable evidence over time, low trust, easy generation, platform weaknesses | **People:** review/support overload and skill erosion. **Process:** larger change batches and weak ownership. **Technology:** security, debt and instability. **Governance:** “built” is mistaken for accepted and supportable. |
| RF-07 | Token bills rise or vary without a clear cost per customer or operational outcome | Immediate: calls, context and agent loops lack attribution. Root: financial, product and technical evidence are not connected. | Provider pricing variance, shared keys, dynamic routing, retries and long context | **People:** no owner can act on cost. **Process:** no unit economics or review trigger. **Technology:** wrong model/tool is retained. **Supplier:** lock-in and price change. **Risk:** loss of viability or service quality through blunt cost cutting. |
| RF-08 | Several “diverse” services fail together or fallback uses the same hidden provider | Immediate: contracts are inventoried supplier by supplier. Root: dependencies are not mapped to critical outcomes and common underlying services. | Cloud/SaaS concentration, opaque subcontracting, fourth parties, weak exit tests | **People/process:** unclear continuity ownership. **Technology:** correlated single points. **Supplier:** weak information rights. **Governance/risk:** concentration is invisible until disruption. |
| RF-09 | Incident updates are late, inconsistent or unusable and exercises exclude key suppliers | Immediate: communication and testing stop at the organisational boundary. Root: joint response is not designed as an end-to-end operational capability. | Contract ambiguity, status competition, cross-border incidents, untested playbooks | **People:** role and message conflicts. **Process:** delay, duplicate reporting and missed thresholds. **Supplier/technology:** incomplete impact data. **Governance:** accountability and regulator/customer duties are not met. |

Across all findings, the deeper problem is the same: **organisations manage agents, AI spend, software and suppliers as separate technology objects when the operational consequence sits in a connected journey and value system**. Inventory without outcome mapping becomes administration; human review without evidence becomes ceremony; observability without intervention becomes retrospective visibility; and supplier assurance without tested continuity becomes a document rather than resilience.

## 6. Methodology defence

Applied proportionately, the approved methodology would defend an organisation as follows:

1. **Identify the problem outside-in.** Start with the customer, service user or stakeholder outcome affected by an agent, AI workflow, internal application or supplier—not with a model, token dashboard or contract list.
2. **Classify and prioritise the work.** Separate assistance, rules-based automation and goal-directed action; classify the service and dependency criticality; compare impact, evidence, urgency, reversibility and affected people.
3. **Name ownership.** Assign an end-to-end human owner for the outcome, local responsibilities for operation and monitoring, specialist identity/security/finance/procurement roles, and the authority that may approve delegation or accept residual exposure.
4. **Make the decision explicit.** Decide the intended value, minimum outcome, permitted delegation, approval thresholds, budget/benefit basis, dependency tolerance and credible no-change or manual alternative.
5. **Apply the connected practices.** Use the operational lenses and interface contract to map work, decisions, tools, data, providers, fourth parties, costs and failure routes; use the readiness path to expose missing evidence; use OPERATE to redesign and implement.
6. **Introduce controls that can operate.** Examples include unique identities, least-privilege tools, reversible action classes, approval gates, cost and call budgets, provider/account/key ownership, action logs, alert thresholds, supplier notification terms, continuity and exit routes.
7. **Manage people and capability.** Define meaningful review, operator competence, absence/offboarding cover, automation-bias countermeasures, workload created by checking, and the capability to operate manually or recover when technology is unavailable.
8. **Handle technology and suppliers as one value system.** Treat models, orchestration, internal software, cloud, identity, data and communications as dependencies on the outcome; identify common providers and changes that alter the control boundary.
9. **Connect risk and resilience.** Link the operational decision to living risk records, current controls, residual exposure, treatment authority and review triggers. A rejected control does not close the risk.
10. **Measure success at outcome level.** Combine service/outcome measures, error and harm, human effort, downstream rework, unit cost, availability, control performance and feedback. Tokens, generated code, agent tasks and adoption counts are diagnostic inputs, not success by themselves.
11. **Test and learn.** Exercise normal, exceptional, adversarial and degraded conditions; include the supplier and receiving teams where the interface is material; test stop, rollback, manual fallback, incident communication and recovery.
12. **Retain proof.** Keep the scope, owner, version, dependency map, decision, permissions, tests, runtime evidence, incidents, costs, outcome review and lesson. Return to an earlier readiness position if evidence weakens.

This is a defensible operational response because it already connects value, people, process, technology, suppliers, evidence, risk and authority. Its limitation is not missing principles; it is the absence of a sufficiently usable artefact set and validated cases.

## 8. Challenges

No equivalent formal challenge was found in the repository. The four challenges below consolidate the known gaps and the new evidence; they do not convert every research term into a methodology component.

### CH-001 — Operationalise agent governance from design to retirement

**Challenge statement:** Operations Automated identifies the right agentic readiness controls but cannot yet guide a practitioner to create and assure a complete agent lifecycle: inventory, owner, identity, delegated authority, action boundaries, approval gates, runtime monitoring, incident response, change, offboarding and retirement.

**Evidence:** RF-01–RF-03; S01–S05. The evidence strengthens the approved readiness path and the guide's declared gaps rather than contradicting either.

**Why this matters:** A paper owner, log store or human review click can create false assurance while an agent acts through excessive, shared or orphaned authority. Failure can affect customers, data, funds, systems and third parties before a periodic review occurs.

**Impacted methodology areas:** human-led automation; ownership, risk, information and technology lenses; Agentic-ready position; OPERATE Automate/Test/Evolve; security/identity, technology, people and assurance interfaces; activation and learning.

**Impacted products/packages:** core method terminology only if later clarification is needed; AI/agent guidance; templates and playbooks; training; assessments; consulting package; future AI Workbench assurance records. It does not imply a new autonomous product.

**Type:** Implementation weakness; tooling gap; training gap; measurement gap; governance gap; emerging capability.

**Priority:** **High.** Severity high; likelihood rising; breadth broad across agent deployments; urgency immediate; regulatory significance medium and increasing; commercial significance high; evidence confidence high for the control set and medium for the best implementation pattern; cost of delay is unmanaged deployment or a later expensive retrofit.

**Proposed outcome:** For a named agent in a named environment, a user can demonstrate who owns the outcome, what the agent may do, which identity and permissions it uses, what requires approval, what is observed, how it is stopped and recovered, and how authority is removed.

**Acceptance criteria:**

1. One proportionate lifecycle record links goal, outcome, owner, identity, tools, data, permissions, action classes, approvals, limits, logs, incidents, changes and retirement.
2. Permission and approval evidence can be tested against actual runtime events.
3. The stop mechanism is external to the agent and a recovery exercise succeeds.
4. Owner absence, role change, offboarding and tool/permission change trigger review or revocation.
5. A normal, adverse, prompt-injection, degraded and unauthorised-action case is tested.
6. An independent reviewer can identify residual uncertainty and challenge a ceremonial control.

**Dependencies:** security and identity expertise; Human-AI Collaboration validation; assurance independence; operational AI monitoring; a non-confidential agent case; any applicable law or sector rule.

**Recommended next action:** **Create or revise implementation guidance**, then test it through a real bounded case. Do not amend the core method until the case shows that the current meaning is insufficient.

### CH-002 — Make AI use, internal software, cost and value governable as one portfolio

**Challenge statement:** Operations Automated cannot yet give a practitioner a usable inventory and measurement route connecting AI tools, provider accounts, keys, workflows, internally created software, owners, dependencies, full costs and realised outcomes. It therefore cannot reliably expose shadow AI, compare models/tools or decide when to scale, change or stop.

**Evidence:** RF-04–RF-07; S06–S12. DSIT's survey limitation and weak benefit attribution, mixed software-productivity evidence and practitioner cost data all point to connected visibility rather than another adoption score.

**Why this matters:** Unseen tools create data and supplier risk; local time savings can transfer cost into review and support; agent calls can multiply spend; and rapid internal software creation can produce unsupported services. Without outcome-level evidence, adoption and token reduction can both make the operation worse.

**Impacted methodology areas:** user-defined value; flow/dependency, information, technology and performance lenses; readiness; OPERATE Observe/Prioritise/Test/Evolve; Finance, Technology, Product, Procurement and Data interfaces; coverage-model inventory, financial resources and lifecycle capabilities.

**Impacted products/packages:** templates; AI value diagnostic; model/tool decision aid; training; assessments; consulting; future Workbench inventory and cost/value evidence. It does not approve a FinOps product or provider connection.

**Type:** Methodology implementation weakness; measurement gap; product gap; tooling gap; training gap.

**Priority:** **High.** Severity medium-high; likelihood high; breadth very broad; urgency immediate; regulatory significance medium where data/outsourcing rules apply; commercial significance high; evidence confidence high that visibility/value gaps exist and medium on standardised AI unit measures; cost of delay is portfolio sprawl and unprovable value.

**Proposed outcome:** Every material AI-enabled workflow or internal AI-built service can be connected to its outcome, owner, users, provider/model/tool, data/access, lifecycle, total cost, comparison baseline, benefits, harms and scale/stop decision.

**Acceptance criteria:**

1. The inventory has clear inclusion and materiality rules and records approved, experimental, embedded and discovered shadow use without implying approval.
2. At least four discovery sources—procurement/expense, identity/access, technology/network or API records, and practitioner declaration—can be reconciled proportionately.
3. One use case reports full cost per outcome unit, not tokens alone, including human review, engineering/support and downstream rework where material.
4. A model/tool selection compares quality, risk, latency, cost, portability, support and non-AI alternatives.
5. An internally built application has a named service owner, acceptance, support, recovery, change and retirement route.
6. A scale/change/stop review uses observed outcomes and unintended effects against a baseline.

**Dependencies:** Finance and technology collaboration; information and access boundaries; cost data; benefit owner; product/service lifecycle guidance; a non-confidential use case.

**Recommended next action:** **Create a template or control** and run a facilitated AI workflow unit-economics case before considering Workbench automation.

### CH-003 — Make critical dependency and concentration mapping practical

**Challenge statement:** Operations Automated recognises dependencies, concentration, lock-in, assurance and exit, but cannot yet produce a proportionate map showing which critical outcomes depend on the same provider, platform, identity service, model, data source or key fourth party and whether alternatives are genuinely independent.

**Evidence:** RF-08 and the AI supply-chain elements of RF-04/RF-07; S05 and S13–S16.

**Why this matters:** Supplier-by-supplier assurance can miss correlated failure. A nominal fallback may depend on the same cloud region, identity provider, model family, telecommunications route or subcontractor. Disruption then affects several services simultaneously and makes contractual exit unusable.

**Impacted methodology areas:** outside-in value system; flow/dependency and resilience lenses; connected work/risk; Controlled and Evolving readiness; OPERATE Observe/Examine/Test; Procurement, Technology, Data, Risk and Assurance interfaces.

**Impacted products/packages:** supplier-management guidance; dependency/concentration template; assessment and assurance package; possible future Workbench relationship view. No supplier connection or data collection is authorised.

**Type:** Implementation weakness; tooling gap; governance gap; resilience gap; measurement gap.

**Priority:** **High.** Severity high for critical services; likelihood medium-high; breadth broad; urgency high in regulated and concentrated technology environments; regulatory significance high in financial services; commercial significance medium-high; evidence confidence high; cost of delay is correlated disruption and costly emergency migration.

**Proposed outcome:** A user can see outcome-to-provider-to-nth-party relationships, common-provider concentration, substitution constraints, assurance evidence, incident dependencies and a tested or explicitly limited continuity/exit route.

**Acceptance criteria:**

1. Critical outcomes and minimum service are defined before suppliers are ranked.
2. Material technology, data, identity, model, communications and support dependencies are linked end to end.
3. Known fourth parties, common providers, locations/regions and correlated failure assumptions are visible with confidence and source.
4. Concentration is assessed by consequence and substitutability, not supplier count alone.
5. Contractual information, audit, change, incident, continuity and exit rights are recorded or identified as gaps.
6. At least one common-provider or unusable-fallback scenario is tested.

**Dependencies:** supplier and architecture evidence; contractual authority; risk ownership; specialist legal/security input; CH-004 for testing and communication.

**Recommended next action:** **Create or revise implementation guidance** and apply it to one bounded critical service.

### CH-004 — Define joint resilience testing and supplier incident communication evidence

**Challenge statement:** Operations Automated requires adverse/degraded testing, interface recovery and retained learning, but cannot yet specify how an organisation and material supplier jointly exercise a disruption or what timely, decision-useful incident communication and evidence should contain.

**Evidence:** RF-09; S05 and S13–S16. The FCA reports material third-party involvement in its 2025 cyber incidents and 2026 rules strengthen reporting expectations.

**Why this matters:** An internal plan cannot prove the supplier will participate, disclose impact, support a workaround or communicate at the pace required by customers, regulators and operational decisions. Untested communication produces delay and conflicting messages when consequence is highest.

**Impacted methodology areas:** connected work and incident records; risk/resilience, people and information lenses; OPERATE Test/Evolve; cross-functional interface contract; Procurement, Communications, Technology, Risk and Assurance interfaces; learning through failure.

**Impacted products/packages:** incident and supplier playbooks; training; assurance; consulting; the raw Incident Management Simulation Game idea; future product capability only after service validation.

**Type:** Implementation weakness; training gap; product opportunity; evidence and assurance weakness; governance gap.

**Priority:** **High** for critical services and **Medium** otherwise. Severity high; likelihood medium-high; breadth targeted to material dependencies; urgency high for regulated firms approaching March 2027; regulatory significance high in financial services; commercial significance high for a facilitated offer; evidence confidence high; cost of delay is an untested response and missed obligations.

**Proposed outcome:** Material organisations and suppliers can jointly demonstrate detection, escalation, impact assessment, decision authority, communication, continuity, recovery and learning against a plausible severe scenario.

**Acceptance criteria:**

1. The exercise begins from a critical outcome, impact tolerance and mapped supplier dependency.
2. Named roles cover customer, operational, supplier, security, risk, legal/regulatory and communication responsibilities where relevant.
3. The playbook defines trigger, recipients, minimum update content, confidence/unknowns, cadence, approval and fallback channel.
4. A supplier or realistic proxy participates; assumptions about unavailable evidence are made visible.
5. Recovery and communication performance are observed, not self-certified from the plan.
6. Lessons create owned actions and a re-test trigger.

**Dependencies:** CH-003 map; incident-management guidance; supplier participation; confidentiality and information-sharing terms; the OA-IDEA-001 review.

**Recommended next action:** **Test through a real operational case** using the proposed RP-04 facilitated tabletop before deciding whether to scope a simulation product.

## 9. Redesign and improvement proposals

All proposals remain review candidates. None changes canonical methodology.

### RP-01 — Agent Delegation and Runtime Assurance Profile

- **Problem:** CH-001; v0.7 requirements are difficult to apply and evidence consistently.
- **Proposed change:** create supporting guidance and a proportionate profile covering agent purpose, owner, identity, action/autonomy classes, tools/data, permissions, approval gates, budgets, logs, stop/recovery, incidents, changes and retirement. Include a short agent charter, runtime-event minimum and exercise record.
- **Why preferable:** it operationalises the existing readiness position without adding a competing method or pretending that one vendor's control plane is universal.
- **Affected content:** automation/AI/agents guide; operational assessment; coverage/interface map; possible cross-links from readiness and OPERATE. Core wording should change only if testing exposes ambiguity.
- **Unintended consequences/complexity risk:** a large register could become ceremonial; logging may capture sensitive data; action tiers could imply false precision; a template could be mistaken for technical enforcement.
- **Tool-agnostic and location:** tool-agnostic supporting guidance and assurance asset, not a core methodology stage.
- **Product/package effect:** potential Workbench record and diagnostic after manual validation.
- **Test:** one low-consequence and one materially consequential non-confidential agent scenario, including offboarding and kill-switch recovery.
- **Accept if:** another practitioner can identify actual authority, reconstruct an action and intervene/recover without the designer's tacit knowledge.
- **Reject or revise if:** it duplicates ordinary IAM/change/incident controls without adding an agent-specific operational decision, or its record burden exceeds the consequence.

### RP-02 — AI Work, Cost and Value Ledger

- **Problem:** CH-002; unseen use, shared cost and activity metrics prevent defensible decisions.
- **Proposed change:** create one lightweight ledger joining AI/automation inventory, internal software lifecycle, provider/model/tool, data/access, owner, baseline, total cost, outcome unit, benefits/harms and scale/change/stop review. Add a category-first model/tool decision aid.
- **Why preferable:** it joins operational value and financial evidence rather than creating separate shadow-AI, token and application registers.
- **Affected content:** value matrix; operational assessment; Finance/Technology/Procurement interfaces; automation guide; coverage map.
- **Unintended consequences/complexity risk:** surveillance of individual experimentation; double counting; false attribution; optimisation of cheap tokens at the expense of service quality; a portfolio too detailed to maintain.
- **Tool-agnostic and location:** tool-agnostic supporting guidance/template; raw provider data remains optional and permissioned.
- **Product/package effect:** manual diagnostic first; future Workbench import or dashboards require a separate connection/data decision.
- **Test:** one AI workflow and one AI-built internal application with a baseline and 30- to 60-day outcome review.
- **Accept if:** the ledger exposes at least one decision that adoption or token totals alone would miss and remains maintainable by the owner.
- **Reject or revise if:** reliable cost/outcome attribution is disproportionate, or existing financial/asset systems can already produce the same governed decision with a small cross-link.

### RP-03 — Critical Dependency and Concentration Map

- **Problem:** CH-003; current dependency guidance does not reliably expose common providers and unusable fallbacks.
- **Proposed change:** extend the existing operational coverage and interface map with critical outcome, provider, key fourth party, common underlying service, location/region, substitutability, information rights, continuity and exit evidence.
- **Why preferable:** it reuses the approved outside-in and interface model rather than building a procurement-only register.
- **Affected content:** coverage/interface map; supplier guidance; connected work/risk; resilience guidance.
- **Unintended consequences/complexity risk:** suppliers may not disclose subcontractors; inferred links may be treated as facts; exhaustive mapping can become impossible; concentration avoidance can add cost and operational complexity without reducing actual risk.
- **Tool-agnostic and location:** tool-agnostic supplier/resilience guidance; keep specialist regulatory mappings separate.
- **Product/package effect:** a facilitated dependency diagnostic; later relationship visualisation only if it improves decisions.
- **Test:** one critical service and one common-provider failure scenario, with confidence labels for incomplete supplier evidence.
- **Accept if:** the exercise changes a continuity, contract, architecture or acceptance decision and makes uncertainty visible.
- **Reject or revise if:** the map cannot be maintained or does not improve a real decision beyond the existing interface contract.

### RP-04 — Joint Supplier Resilience Exercise and Communication Pack

- **Problem:** CH-004; internal tests and generic communication plans do not prove cross-boundary response.
- **Proposed change:** create a facilitated tabletop pack with scenario design, roles, injects, evidence capture, communication checkpoints, recovery observations, debrief, actions and re-test. Use CH-003's dependency map as the entry condition.
- **Why preferable:** a facilitated service can validate the customer problem and methodology before a multiplayer software product.
- **Affected content:** incident/resilience practice guidance; learning review; supplier interface; later use-case catalogue.
- **Unintended consequences/complexity risk:** participants may game the scenario; supplier absence may create false confidence; performance records could become intrusive; an exercise could be misrepresented as assurance of real resilience.
- **Tool-agnostic and location:** tool-agnostic practice and service asset, not a new core stage.
- **Product/package effect:** directly reassesses OA-IDEA-001. The current next action remains a facilitated low-technology test, not platform scoping.
- **Test:** one 60- to 90-minute non-confidential tabletop followed by an owned action and scheduled re-test.
- **Accept if:** the exercise exposes a decision, ownership, communication, recovery or supplier-evidence weakness that a document review missed.
- **Reject or revise if:** a simpler walkthrough produces the same learning, the exercise creates unsupported assurance, or target customers will not supply the required cross-functional participation.

## 10. Product and commercial opportunities

### PO-01 — AI and Agent Operational Assurance Diagnostic

- **Customer problem:** organisations are piloting agents but cannot show bounded authority, runtime control, recovery or accountable ownership.
- **Target customer:** operations, risk, technology and transformation leaders with a named agent use case; begin with small and medium service/back-office operations or a narrow regulated pilot.
- **Current alternative:** generic AI readiness score, security review, vendor checklist or architecture assessment conducted separately.
- **Proposed value:** one outside-in assessment linking outcome, readiness, permissions, human control, runtime evidence and recovery.
- **Methodology connection:** strongest direct fit with readiness, OPERATE, connected risk and human-led automation.
- **Evidence:** RF-01–RF-03; High control-need confidence, unvalidated willingness to pay.
- **Minimum viable offer/delivery:** a facilitated fixed-scope review producing an agent charter, gaps, control/evidence plan and governed next action.
- **Recurring element:** periodic permission/value review only where ongoing use justifies it.
- **Risks/constraints:** specialist security/legal limits; no certification claim; access to non-confidential but sufficient evidence; quickly changing technology.
- **Confidence and validation:** Medium-high. Run two founding-user cases and test whether a buyer pays for the decision and evidence, not the label.

### PO-02 — AI Workflow Unit Economics and Value Review

- **Customer problem:** leaders can see licences or token totals but not cost per useful outcome, downstream effort or a scale/stop decision.
- **Target customer:** operations/product owners and finance/technology partners running one material AI workflow.
- **Current alternative:** cloud bill dashboards, provider analytics, ROI spreadsheets or broad AI strategy work.
- **Proposed value:** connect baseline, total cost, quality, human effort, rework, risk and outcome in one decision-ready review.
- **Methodology connection:** user-defined value, performance lens, readiness and Finance interface.
- **Evidence:** RF-05–RF-07; strong problem signal, immature common measures.
- **Minimum viable offer/delivery:** a 30- to 60-day facilitated measurement sprint using an agreed outcome unit and scale/change/stop review.
- **Recurring element:** quarterly value review if the workflow remains material and variable.
- **Risks/constraints:** attribution may be weak; small users may lack data; blunt cost reduction can damage outcomes.
- **Confidence and validation:** Medium. Test with one workflow and require an actual decision changed by the evidence.

### PO-03 — Critical AI and Technology Dependency Resilience Review

- **Customer problem:** an organisation cannot see common providers, key fourth parties or whether fallback and exit are viable.
- **Target customer:** service owners, supplier managers and risk/resilience teams with one critical service.
- **Current alternative:** supplier due diligence, business continuity review or contract register conducted separately.
- **Proposed value:** outcome-led dependency and concentration map plus one tested failure scenario and prioritised treatment.
- **Methodology connection:** outside-in system, dependency lens, supplier interface, connected work/risk and Test.
- **Evidence:** RF-08–RF-09; high in regulated financial services, plausible elsewhere.
- **Minimum viable offer/delivery:** fixed-scope facilitated assessment for one service; specialist regulatory mapping optional and separately qualified.
- **Recurring element:** review after material supplier/architecture change and periodic scenario re-test.
- **Risks/constraints:** incomplete supplier transparency; potential overlap with mature third-party risk functions; confidential data boundary.
- **Confidence and validation:** Medium-high for the problem, Medium for the proposed market. Interview five target practitioners and run one case.

### PO-04 — Joint Incident Simulation and Learning Service

- **Customer problem:** teams and suppliers have plans but lack observable, repeatable cross-functional practice.
- **Target customer:** teams responsible for a critical customer service with a material technology or supplier dependency.
- **Current alternative:** static tabletop, cyber exercise, major-incident training or supplier assurance questionnaire.
- **Proposed value:** Operations Automated connects the scenario to the primary journey, minimum outcome, authority, communication, recovery and retained improvement.
- **Methodology connection:** learning through failure, OPERATE Test/Evolve, connected work/risk and supplier interfaces.
- **Evidence:** RF-09 and OA-IDEA-001; need is credible, differentiation and willingness to pay unvalidated.
- **Minimum viable offer/delivery:** facilitator-led tabletop, lightweight evidence capture and re-test plan; no multiplayer platform.
- **Recurring element:** scenario library and periodic re-test only after the service proves value.
- **Risks/constraints:** crowded market; false assurance; participant time; sensitive incident details; evaluator competence.
- **Confidence and validation:** Medium. There are existing ideas related to this area. Review them before finalising the scope. Reassess OA-IDEA-001 after a paid or strongly committed facilitated pilot.

The opportunities should be validated in this order: PO-01 or PO-02 first for the narrowest evidence-producing offer; PO-03 where an accessible critical-service case exists; PO-04 as a facilitated test of the existing idea. Building Workbench features before these services produce repeated value would conflict with the approved delivery and commercial strategy.

## 14. Final assurance statement

Operations Automated **partially addresses the research and requires implementation improvements, not material redesign, on current evidence**.

Its strongest current position is that delegation and technology are never the goal: value, human authority, readiness, monitoring, recovery and learning must be established for the specific operation. The external evidence supports that position. Its most material weakness is that the approved method often tells a practitioner what must be true without yet supplying a proportionate, tested way to evidence it across the lifecycle.

The four proposed challenges are specific, consolidated and testable. The redesign proposals remain tool-agnostic and primarily belong in supporting guidance, assurance and facilitated delivery. Core methodology change should be considered only if real cases show that the present principles or component boundaries cannot produce the required result.

The most important unresolved question is:

> What is the smallest evidence set that lets a competent outsider verify an AI-enabled operation's value, delegated authority, runtime control and dependency resilience without creating a ceremonial register that the operation cannot maintain?

Jamie is invited to identify what is wrong, missing, impractical or inconsistent in this methodology response.
