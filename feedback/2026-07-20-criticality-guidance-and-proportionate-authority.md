# Criticality guidance and proportionate authority

## Source and boundary

- **Source:** Jamie Peppard's answer to the transfer test concerning a small care provider without a formal resilience function.
- **Scenario:** A hypothetical organisation had classified a scheduling service as non-critical despite potential material harm to customers if it failed.
- **Permission to use:** Jamie continued the founder challenge exercise and reframed the primary question.
- **Confidentiality boundary:** General methodology reasoning and public-framework comparison only.

## Correction to the scenario

The central problem was not deciding whether management or frontline staff should win a disagreement. The criticality assessment was defective because it considered the service primarily through an internal business or financial lens.

A service, outcome or activity should be considered for criticality when disruption could cause material harm to:

- Customers or service users
- Third parties and dependent organisations
- Employees or other affected people
- The organisation's ability to deliver essential outcomes
- Applicable legal, safety, ethical, contractual or regulatory interests

The potential harm makes the hypothetical scheduling service a candidate critical service even if management originally described it as internal administration.

## Portable good practice

Operations Automated should learn from strong practices in FCA, PRA, ITIL and other relevant frameworks without copying their complete regulatory or organisational implementation into every context.

The methodology may guide users to:

- Identify services, outcomes and dependencies whose disruption could cause material harm
- Define tolerable disruption and minimum outcomes
- Map people, processes, information, technology, facilities and third parties
- Test severe but plausible failure
- Establish ownership, escalation, approval and retained learning

These are good-practice reasoning patterns. The depth, terminology, governance structure, evidence and approval route should remain proportionate to the user, team, organisation, consequence and applicable obligations.

Operations Automated must not imply that following its guidance demonstrates FCA, PRA, ITIL or other external compliance or certification.

## Scale from individual to organisation

The methodology is not only for whole organisations.

- An individual may identify a critical personal outcome, activity or dependency and improve the controls within their authority.
- A team may identify a critical local service, process or dependency and prepare evidence, controls and recommendations.
- A wider organisation may reconcile local assessments, set shared tolerances, allocate investment and accept material residual risk.

The methodology should focus on the decision and the authority it requires rather than prescribe titles such as manager, committee or board in every context.

Where a proposed control materially affects cost, risk, customer outcomes or another area outside the user's authority, the user should prepare the evidence and obtain a decision from the person or group authorised for that consequence.

## Non-negotiable reasoning and adaptable implementation

Jamie's position establishes an important distinction:

- The methodology should be firm that material harm, affected people, dependencies and applicable obligations are considered.
- It should remain flexible about organisational structure, terminology, documentation depth and how the practice is implemented.

This resembles the use of a good-practice framework: the method gives a defensible route and useful options, but it does not demand identical implementation in every environment.

## Remaining challenges

1. **Local fragmentation:** several teams may independently label services critical using inconsistent boundaries or tolerances.
2. **Authority ambiguity:** identifying criticality, validating evidence, funding controls and accepting residual risk may require different authority.
3. **Rejected local concern:** a team may identify credible material harm but the authorised investment decision-maker may decline remediation.
4. **Guidance boundary:** if consideration of material harm is essential to methodology integrity, it may need stronger language than an entirely optional suggestion even though implementation remains adaptable.
5. **External-method use:** borrowed reasoning should be attributed and translated without creating a false claim of compliance.

## Disposition

**Contextual convergence, with an authority-pattern clarification candidate.**

Test a decision pattern that separates:

1. Identifying a possible critical service or outcome
2. Assessing evidence, harm, dependencies and options
3. Deciding the priority and tolerance
4. Approving investment or accepting residual risk

The pattern should define authority by consequence rather than prescribe job titles.

## Retained lesson

Criticality follows potential material harm and essential outcomes, not only profit or the label attached to a process. Use strong external practices as adaptable guidance, and send consequential decisions to the authority appropriate to their impact.
